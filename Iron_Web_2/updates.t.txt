DIFFERENTIATORS:

1. The "Wait, this conflicts with your trip" engine
Your hotel example is one instance of a broader system: detect contradictions across a trip, not just within a single booking.

Examples:
- Two hotels booked for overlapping nights.
- Hotel checkout is at 11 AM, but the next hotel check-in is at 3 PM, with luggage and transport unaccounted for.
- A flight lands after the hotel check-in desk closes.
- The user schedules an attraction on the day they're supposed to travel to another city.
- A tour starts at 9 AM, but the user arrives at the destination at 10:30 AM.
- The return flight is from a different city than the itinerary's final stop.
Why it impresses: The website understands the trip as one connected plan, rather than a collection of isolated reservations.

Implementation: A rule engine that runs whenever a booking or itinerary item is added, moved or changed.

2. Constraint-aware destination search
P0

This is one of the highest-value additions to your existing AI.

Convert the request into structured fields:
- Origin and destination preferences.
- Departure and return dates, or trip duration.
- Total budget and number of travelers.
- Group composition: solo, couple, family, friends.
- Preferred activities and travel pace.
- Hard requirements and soft preferences.
Logic: Apply hard filters first; rank the remaining options by soft preferences. If no destination satisfies every constraint, explain why and offer the smallest useful compromise rather than returning fabricated matches.




3. Itinerary generation and validation
P0

Turn a destination recommendation into a usable day-by-day trip.

Logic:
- Assign attractions to days based on opening hours, duration and proximity when those data are available.
- Estimate activity durations and travel time between stops.
- Respect the trip's start/end dates and daily activity limits.
- Avoid impossible sequences, duplicate activities and excessive backtracking.
- Leave room for meals, rest and travel.
- Handle arrival and departure days differently from full days.
An LLM can suggest the itinerary, but a validator should reject or repair schedules that violate known constraints.




4. Budget estimation engine
P0

A travel website should be able to answer: "Can I actually afford this trip?"

Estimate:
- Transport to and from the destination.
- Local transport.
- Accommodation per night.
- Food per person per day.
- Activities and entrance fees.
- Miscellaneous expenses and an optional contingency buffer.
Logic: Calculate the total, cost per traveler, cost per day and remaining budget. If the itinerary exceeds the budget, suggest concrete changes: cheaper accommodation, fewer paid activities, a shorter stay or a different destination.

Label estimates as estimates. For the hackathon, use seeded sample prices with clear assumptions and a date/source field if available.




5. Trip and package comparison
P0

Let users compare complete trip options rather than just destination descriptions.

Compare estimated total cost, travel duration, number of activities, pace, accommodation standard, travel effort and fit with stated preferences.

Logic: Normalize each option into the same schema and calculate a transparent score. Show the trade-offs too; the cheapest trip is not necessarily the best match.




6. Trip lifecycle and persistence
P0

A recommendation should become an editable trip, not disappear when the chat ends.

Logic: Create, save, retrieve, duplicate, revise and delete trips. Track statuses such as draft, planned and completed. Save the selected destinations, itinerary version, cost estimates and user edits.

A mock booking flow can be a separate state such as demo_reserved, explicitly labeled as a simulation. Do not imply that a real reservation exists.


3. The features that make it feel like a real product
Once the six core systems work, add these in roughly this order.
Feature	Backend logic
Weather and season suitability — P1	Score destinations for the travel dates using a real source or clearly labeled sample seasonal data.
Nearby attraction discovery — P1	Find attractions within a radius or travel-time limit; group them geographically.
Transport feasibility — P1	Estimate routes, duration and cost between origin, destination and itinerary stops.
Accommodation recommendations — P1	Filter mock stays by nightly budget, guest count, amenities and location.
Collaborative trip planning — P1	Shared trip members, comments, votes and a selected itinerary.
Alternatives and replanning — P1	Swap a destination or activity and recompute schedule, cost and compatibility.
Travel advisories — P1	Surface sourced warnings, entry requirements or permit constraints where relevant.
Demo reservation workflow — P1	Validate guest details, calculate a mock total and store a clearly simulated reservation.
Packing checklist — P2	Generate a checklist from season, activities, trip duration and traveler needs.
Trip readiness score — P2	Flag missing dates, unallocated budget, travel gaps and unresolved constraints.
Personalized post-trip learning — P2	Use feedback to improve future ranking and saved preferences.
Don't build every row before the demo. A working itinerary engine, budget calculator and saved-trip workflow are much stronger than twelve superficial AI features.
4. The actual backend data model
This is where I'd start coding. Your database should represent the travel domain independently of the agent's memory.

A practical relational schema, whether you're using PostgreSQL, Supabase or another database:
users

Identity and account preferences


user_preferences

Budget range, interests, pace, accommodation preferences


destinations

Geography, categories, seasons, cost ranges


attractions

Activities, duration, coordinates, hours, entrance fees


accommodations

Sample stays, nightly price, capacity, amenities


transport_options

Mock routes, mode, duration, estimated fare


trips

Owner, dates, origin, status, budget, selected destination


trip_days

Date, day number, daily notes and planned budget


itinerary_items

Attraction, order, time slot, duration, cost, travel gap


trip_estimates

Cost breakdown, assumptions, estimate timestamp


trip_members

Collaborators and permissions, if needed


trip_feedback

Ratings, edits, reasons for accepting or rejecting options


demo_reservations

Simulated reservation details and explicit demo status



Important implementation decisions:
- Use IDs and foreign keys rather than embedding whole destination objects into trip records.
- Store prices with currency, pricing unit and an indication of whether they are estimated or sourced.
- Store dates separately from trip duration; validate that the return date isn't before departure.
- Save the generated itinerary as structured data, not only as an LLM response string.
- Track itinerary revisions so regenerating a trip doesn't silently destroy user edits.
- Keep AI conversation memory separate from the canonical trip record. The database is the source of truth for dates, budgets, saved plans and trip status.
5. API endpoints your frontend should call
You don't need dozens of microservices. Start with a modular backend and endpoints that correspond to user actions.
Endpoint	Responsibility
POST /api/travel/plan	Interpret a request and generate ranked trip options
GET /api/destinations	Search and filter destinations
GET /api/destinations/:id	Return destination details and attractions
POST /api/trips	Save a selected trip
GET /api/trips	List a user's saved trips
GET /api/trips/:id	Load the complete trip
PATCH /api/trips/:id	Update dates, budget, destination or preferences
POST /api/trips/:id/itinerary/generate	Generate or regenerate an itinerary
PATCH /api/trips/:id/itinerary	Apply user edits to the itinerary
POST /api/trips/:id/budget/recalculate	Recompute trip costs
POST /api/trips/:id/recommendations	Find alternatives or upgrades
POST /api/trips/:id/demo-reservations	Create a simulated reservation
POST /api/trips/:id/feedback	Save feedback to improve recommendations
Add validation, authorization and consistent error responses to every write endpoint. For example, a user should never be able to edit another user's trip simply by changing its ID.
6. How your partner's AI agents should fit into this
This part matters because you already have a functioning agentic system. Don't replace it. Give it access to actual travel capabilities.

I'd divide responsibilities like this, adapting them to the agents your partner already has.

Intent agent

Understands requests such as "a quiet five-day mountain trip under ₹25,000." Produces structured constraints, resolves ambiguity and consults memory for relevant preferences.



Discovery agent

Queries the actual destination and attraction database, combines semantic retrieval with structured filters, and returns candidate IDs and matching reasons.



Itinerary agent

Proposes an ordered schedule using the available attractions, trip duration and user preferences. A separate deterministic validator checks feasibility.



Budget engine

Calculates actual totals from your seeded pricing data. This should primarily be ordinary backend code, not another LLM.



Validation and response layer

Rejects impossible schedules, checks budget compliance, verifies referenced IDs, identifies unmet constraints and returns a clear explanation of trade-offs.



Your existing embedding reranking and query rewriting can remain exactly where they are useful. But semantic similarity shouldn't override hard constraints. A destination can be an excellent semantic match and still be impossible for the requested dates or budget.

. The hidden travel-cost detector
Show the cost of a decision beyond its advertised price.

Example: A hotel costs ₹2,000 less, but it adds ₹800 per day in transport for three days. The cheaper hotel actually costs ₹400 more overall, before accounting for the extra travel time.

Other examples:
- A cheap flight requires an expensive airport transfer.
- A free attraction requires a costly taxi to reach it.
- A hotel saves money but creates an extra night of accommodation due to awkward flight times.
Why it impresses: The system optimizes for total trip cost, not the cheapest individual item.

Implementation: Recalculate transport, accommodation, activities and time costs whenever the user compares or replaces an option. Show which amounts are estimates.

3. The exhaustion-aware itinerary
Most itinerary generators try to maximize the number of attractions. Yours could try to maximize the quality of the trip.

Example: The user has scheduled a 6 AM hike, a two-hour drive, three attractions, and a late-night event on the same day. The system flags the day as demanding and suggests moving one activity.

Detect:
- Too many consecutive high-effort activities.
- Long journeys immediately before early starts.
- No rest or meal windows.
- Several physically demanding days in a row.
- A packed arrival day after an overnight journey.
Why it impresses: It understands that a technically feasible itinerary can still be miserable.

Implementation: Give each activity an estimated duration and effort level; calculate daily load and recovery time. Let users choose relaxed, balanced or packed travel.

4. The "What if this goes wrong?" planner
Give each itinerary a lightweight contingency plan.

Examples:
- If an outdoor activity is unavailable due to rain, suggest a nearby indoor alternative.
- If a stop is skipped, identify which later activities can still fit.
- If the user loses half a day, automatically produce a shortened itinerary.
- If a connection is missed, explain which downstream plans need to be changed.
Why it impresses: The system doesn't just plan an ideal trip; it helps the traveler recover when reality differs from the plan.

Implementation: Store alternative activities by location, category, duration and cost. A "replan" action should preserve the user's non-negotiables and recalculate the schedule.


5. The "You said you wanted a relaxing trip" reminder
Use the agentic memory system to detect when the user's decisions contradict their stated preferences.

Example: The user initially asks for a peaceful holiday with minimal travel. Later, they select four cities in five days.

The site says: "You've prioritized fewer transfers and a relaxed pace. This version includes three hotel changes. Want to see a slower alternative?"

Another example: a user says they want to keep the total under ₹20,000, then selects an accommodation that pushes the trip to ₹24,500.

Why it impresses: Your existing memory system becomes useful in a concrete, visible way.

Implementation: Compare each action against explicit user preferences and trip constraints. Explain the conflict and offer options rather than blocking the user.

Every sensible TinyFish integration point
I'd split the integrations into three categories: enriching your database, researching a particular trip, and keeping the agent's knowledge current. That gives you many legitimate TinyFish touchpoints without shoehorning it into basic calculations.
1. Destination database enrichment
These are especially valuable because they turn your Geofabrik import into a proper travel-intelligence database.

1. Ideal travel season and seasonal caveats P0


Research official tourism sources and relevant local authorities to identify suitable months, monsoon disruptions, extreme heat, snow closures and seasonal accessibility.

TinyFish: Search → Fetch. Save the source and verification date.



2. Activities and experiences available P0


Find what visitors can actually do: trekking, rafting, wildlife safaris, heritage walks, stargazing, boating, food tours, cultural experiences and so on. Distinguish confirmed activities from generic destination descriptions.



3. Realistic visit duration P0


Research whether an attraction needs an hour, half a day or a full day; whether a destination warrants a weekend or a longer stay; and whether travel time makes a day trip impractical.



4. Permits, restrictions and entry requirements P0


Check protected-area permits, restricted regions, foreign-national requirements where applicable, booking prerequisites, visitor caps and rules for specific activities. Prefer official government and operator sources.



5. Accessibility and practical suitability P0


Investigate wheelchair access, stairs, steep paths, road access, availability of facilities and other constraints. Record unknown when no credible evidence exists instead of inventing accessibility claims.



6. Altitude, weather and physical difficulty

Research altitude-related risks, trek grades, expected walking effort, weather hazards and whether acclimatisation may be relevant. This is particularly useful for mountain destinations.



7. Crowd patterns and quieter alternatives

Find documented peak periods, festival surges, popular visiting hours and less-crowded nearby alternatives. Treat crowd claims as estimates unless reliable visitor data is available.



8. Cost evidence, not budget arithmetic

Research current published attraction entry fees, permits, activity prices and transport fares. Your own code should add these values, estimate the total and compare it with the user's budget.



9. How to reach a destination

Find relevant railheads, airports, bus connections, last-mile transport, seasonal road restrictions and transfer options. Use actual schedules or official operator information where available.



10. Nearby destinations and practical combinations

Discover worthwhile nearby places that aren't obvious from geographic proximity alone. Verify whether a road connection exists and whether the combination is realistic in one trip.



11. Food and dietary practicality

Research local food specialties, vegetarian availability, vegan options and documented dietary constraints. This can enrich your destination metadata, although individual restaurant availability belongs in trip-specific research.



12. Facilities and emergency-relevant information

Verify the availability of visitor facilities, official park contacts, public transport information and nearby medical facilities where relevant. Store source dates; don't imply a facility is currently open merely because a webpage lists it.



How to implement this layer: don't send every imported OSM object through TinyFish. First filter for genuine travel destinations, prioritise the places likely to be shortlisted, and enrich missing or stale fields. Batch compatible research where possible, but retain separate evidence for each claim.
2. Live trip research
This is the user-facing layer. Your database provides the candidates; TinyFish investigates the shortlist when the user actually plans a trip.

13. Accommodation research P0


Research properties matching the group's location, price range, room requirements, Wi-Fi needs, accessibility constraints and preferred atmosphere. Extract published prices, policies and amenities with sources.



14. Transport option research P0


Find relevant train, bus, flight or operator pages and compare routes, schedules, transfer requirements and published fares. Don't claim a seat or fare is available without current evidence.



15. Attraction tickets and activity bookings P0


Investigate entry fees, ticketing requirements, booking windows, time slots, cancellation rules and operator availability for activities in the proposed itinerary.



16. Trip-date-specific conditions

Check official announcements and destination-specific information for the user's actual travel dates: closures, festivals, access restrictions, weather disruptions and special events. Use a dedicated weather data source for forecasts when appropriate.



17. Restaurant and food research

Find suitable restaurants and local food experiences near the itinerary, checking published hours, dietary options, reservation policies and current menu information where available.



18. Event discovery

Search for concerts, exhibitions, local festivals, cultural performances, markets and seasonal experiences happening during the trip. Verify event dates against organisers' pages.



19. Itinerary feasibility verification

When an itinerary contains several stops, research opening hours, access restrictions, transfer options and visit-duration evidence to identify impossible or rushed days. Let your own code handle basic distance and time calculations.



20. Resolve trade-offs with evidence

If the group is torn between two destinations, investigate the specific difference that matters: crowding, activities, travel effort, accommodation costs, weather suitability or accessibility. This is a targeted investigation, not another generic search.



3. Memory-driven and multi-agent intelligence
This is where I'd put the most effort for your hackathon. These integrations make TinyFish part of your agentic memory architecture, rather than just a data provider.

21. Investigate remembered preferences

If memory says a traveller dislikes commercialised hill stations, prefers quiet places and enjoys long hikes, research destinations and activities against those specific preferences. Don't make TinyFish rediscover preferences your database already knows.



22. Resolve group conflicts

Different group members may have conflicting needs: low cost versus comfort, adventure versus relaxation, nightlife versus early starts. Use TinyFish to gather evidence on the contested options; your agents decide how the group should trade off the results.



23. Revalidate plans after memory changes

If a person joins or leaves, the dates change, or an accessibility requirement is introduced, identify which itinerary claims are affected and rerun only the necessary research.



24. Challenge recommendations with independent evidence

Give a critic agent the current recommendation and ask it to find material counterevidence: hidden fees, inconvenient transfers, closures, misleading amenity claims or unsuitable conditions. Use TinyFish to investigate those claims rather than asking the critic to speculate.



25. Resolve contradictions in memory

If stored information says an attraction is open year-round but a current official notice says it is closed for maintenance, retrieve the newer evidence, flag the conflict and update the appropriate record. Keep historical values and their provenance rather than silently overwriting everything.



26. Learn from rejected options

When a user rejects an otherwise suitable destination, your memory system should store the reason. TinyFish is useful if the reason needs further investigation—for example, determining whether a destination's nightlife is concentrated in a particular area. A plain “I don't like this” does not itself require a web search.



27. Explore alternatives intelligently

When the first-choice destination is too expensive, inaccessible or unsuitable for the dates, use your destination graph to find alternatives and TinyFish to verify the distinguishing facts that make each alternative viable.



28. Build evidence-backed recommendation explanations

For each major recommendation, show which remembered preferences mattered, which external facts support the choice, what remains uncertain and why the system rejected alternatives. TinyFish supplies evidence; your architecture explains its relevance.



4. Ongoing intelligence and database maintenance
These can happen in the background rather than on every user request. They're legitimate uses of web research and especially useful if your architecture supports persistent memory and scheduled agent work.

29. Detect changes to important destination information

Periodically investigate official sources for changes to entry rules, fees, opening hours, permits or access restrictions. Update records only when evidence supports a change.



30. Fill missing metadata on demand

When a user searches for a destination whose seasonality or activities are unknown, trigger research for those missing fields instead of trying to fully annotate every record upfront.



31. Verify high-impact claims

Flag records that lack a credible source, contain conflicting values or have not been checked for a long time. Prioritise facts that could derail a trip over low-impact descriptive details.



32. Detect newly relevant destinations and experiences

Investigate official tourism announcements and newly published destination information to discover experiences absent from your initial catalogue. Add candidates only after matching them to existing entities and checking their provenance.



TinyFish Search is well suited to source discovery and current information; Fetch reads the discovered pages as clean content. Use the Web Agent when a site requires multi-step interaction or dynamic extraction rather than using browser automation for every URL.
